Amplitude Modulation (AM) Generation

1. What is Amplitude Modulation?

Amplitude Modulation is the process in which the amplitude of the high-frequency carrier wave is varied in linear proportion to the instantaneous value of the modulating (message) signal. The frequency and phase of the carrier remain unchanged.

Let modulating signal be: m(t) = Am cos(2π fm t)

Let carrier signal be: c(t) = Ac cos(2π fc t)

Then the resulting AM wave expression is:

s(t) = [Ac + m(t)] cos(2π fc t)
s(t) = Ac [1 + μ cos(2π fm t)] cos(2π fc t)

Where μ = Am / Ac is the Modulation Index (or modulation depth).

2. Modulation States

Under-modulation (μ < 1)

Message amplitude is smaller than carrier amplitude. The envelope of the modulated carrier stays strictly above and below the zero-axis. The message can be easily recovered using a cheap envelope detector.

Critical Modulation (μ = 1)

The modulation envelope touches the zero line precisely. The modulation depth is 100%. This represents the maximum amount of power that can be allocated to sidebands without distortion.

Over-modulation (μ > 1)

Message amplitude is larger than carrier amplitude. The envelope crosses the zero axis, leading to phase reversals in the carrier signal. The original envelope is distorted, rendering diode detectors ineffective (envelope clipping/distortion).

3. Methods of AM Generation

Square-Law Modulator

Suitable for low power generation. Uses a non-linear device (like a diode or FET operating in its quadratic region) combined with a bandpass filter centered at fc.

Switching Modulator

High-power modulation scheme. The carrier signal acts as a switching command that drives a diode bridge or transistor between conducting (ON) and non-conducting (OFF) states, chopping the modulating signal. Passing this chopped signal through a bandpass filter recovers the AM wave.

Amplitude Modulation (AM) Detection (Demodulation)

1. The Envelope Detector Circuit

The simplest and most widely used method to demodulate an AM wave is the Envelope Detector (or Diode Detector). It is a passive circuit consisting of a diode and a low-pass RC filter. It requires no local carrier synchronization, making AM receivers simple and inexpensive.

2. Circuit Operation Step-by-Step

  1. Positive Half Cycles: When the input voltage exceeds the capacitor voltage, the diode is forward-biased (ON). The capacitor charges rapidly through the forward resistance of the diode, tracking the peak of the carrier voltage.
  2. Negative Half Cycles: When the input voltage drops below the capacitor voltage, the diode becomes reverse-biased (OFF). The capacitor discharges slowly through the resistor R. The rate of discharge is governed by the time constant τ = RC.

3. Selecting the Ideal RC Time Constant

Choosing the correct values of R and C is critical for accurate signal recovery. There are two failure states:

Diagonal Clipping (RC is too large)

If the time constant RC is much larger than the maximum rate of change of the modulating envelope, the capacitor cannot discharge fast enough to follow the envelope. The output decays along a flat exponential slope, clipping the peaks of the message wave.

RC > 1 / (2π fm)

Poor Filtering / Ripple (RC is too small)

If the time constant RC is too small, the capacitor discharges extremely fast between carrier peaks. The output voltage returns to zero or drops heavily before the next carrier cycle, producing a heavily rippled output that tracks the carrier frequency instead of the message envelope.

RC < 1 / fc

The Golden Condition for Ideal Detection:

1 / fc << RC << 1 / fm

To prevent diagonal clipping and recover the message with minimum distortion, the RC time constant must satisfy: RC ≤ 1 / (2π fm μ) at maximum modulation index.

Lab Experiments Guide

Experiment 1: Observing Under-Modulation vs Over-Modulation

Explore how the ratio of modulating signal to carrier signal affects the transmitted AM wave.

  1. Select the Simulator tab.
  2. Turn off all channels except the **Message Signal** and **AM Modulated** channels.
  3. Set Carrier Amplitude (Ac) to 3.0 V. Set Message Amplitude (Am) to 1.5 V.
  4. Observe the modulation index μ. It should be 0.5. Observe the envelope of the AM wave. It tracks the message signal perfectly. This is **under-modulation**.
  5. Now increase Message Amplitude (Am) to 4.0 V. Observe μ. It rises above 1.0. Look closely at the center nodes of the AM wave: the phases invert and the envelope crosses zero. This is **over-modulation**. Notice the red warning state on the right telemetry panel.

Experiment 2: Troubleshooting Envelope Detection

Understand the critical role of the resistor-capacitor (RC) time constant in demodulating the signal.

  1. Configure a clean under-modulated wave (e.g. Ac = 3.0 V, Am = 2.0 V, fm = 1.0 Hz, fc = 35 Hz).
  2. Turn on the **Detected Envelope vd(t)** channel checkbox.
  3. Observe the orange curve. It should closely trace the peaks of the AM wave with tiny carrier ripples.
  4. Diagonal Clipping Simulation: Slide the **Resistance (R)** to 2000 Ω and **Capacitance (C)** to 8.0 µF. The RC constant will rise above 15 ms. Look at the oscilloscope screen: the orange line fails to follow the rapid downward slope of the message envelope. It draws straight lines cutting through the wave. This is **diagonal clipping**.
  5. Excessive Ripple Simulation: Drop **Resistance (R)** to 50 Ω and **Capacitance (C)** to 0.2 µF. The time constant drops to 0.01 ms. The output envelope turns into jagged peaks that fall back to zero instantly between carrier cycles. This is **poor filtering**.
  6. Adjust the sliders to find the "Sweet Spot" where the telemetry panel displays: RC time constant is ideal.
